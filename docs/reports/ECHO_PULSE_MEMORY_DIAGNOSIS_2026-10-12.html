Pulse diagnosis: the input is visible, but its latent response has memory

Your pulse proposal was diagnostically useful. It exposes a failure beyond B regression: after a brief input ends, the measured eigenfunction response does not initially follow its fitted free-motion exponential. In a late, well-modelled free trajectory, the long-window response grows to 7,287 times its first-hold value. Extrapolating that first response misses essentially all subsequent response. After the window is entirely post-pulse, a re-anchored exponential predicts it to 0.14–0.20%. A better immediate B cannot, by itself, supply this missing autonomous evolution.

The short window is less affected, not unaffected. Dense pulse calibration retains useful mild control (two eigenfunctions: 6.68% echo error, 11.25% known-driver error) but does not outperform the prior sine-trained fit (5.13%, 8.63%). Nonlinear control is still unsuccessful. The primary pipeline was not changed.

Default pulse width is one hold, 0.01 τ (about 30.1 ns). Amplitude δ is 0.01 for free/mild and 0.1 for nonlinear preparations, with ±δ/2 and ±2δ controls in the long-response campaign. Selected preparations also test widths 0.005 and 0.05 τ. These are finite rectangular pulses, not unit-area Dirac impulses; fixed-amplitude width comparisons also change pulse area. Hardware bandwidth was not modelled. The pulse durations are numerical probes, not a claim that an acoustic laboratory can deliver them unchanged.

The simulator and archived r,v are used only to acquire matched branches. Analysis and regression receive echo prehistory, echo continuations and known inputs—not states or equations. Exact replay is an idealized capability; a laboratory would need reproducible preparations, reset/repetition, measurement-noise averaging and actual actuator calibration. At the pulse start, any preceding sine forcing is switched off; after the finite pulse, input is zero for the entire continuation.

2. A test that removes B-regression error from the argument

At each echo sample the native ring buffer can immediately evaluate the frozen coordinates. With samples ordered oldest to newest, the actual projection is:

ξⱼ,ₖ = σⱼ⁻¹ Σa=0d−1 Ua,j ek−d+1+a,   zⱼ,ₖ = ψⱼ(ξ₁,ₖ, ξ₂,ₖ).

Stored singular-vector and singular-value conventions are reused exactly. There is no new normalization, centering, or SVD in this experiment. Online ring-buffer evaluation and the native batch projection are cross-checked. After initial warm-up, there is no requirement to wait a whole window to read ξ. The issue is how the new echo affects the coordinate as it moves through that window.

For identical initial histories, define the measured, centered finite-difference response to a one-hold pulse:

Gⱼ(ℓh) = [zⱼ+δ(ℓh) − zⱼ−δ(ℓh)] / (2δ),   Gⱼ(0) = 0.

This subtraction cancels the shared zero-input baseline and estimates local causal sensitivity about zero current input. It is not an estimate of the entire response to an arbitrarily large input. Half/double amplitudes test the local linear approximation.

If the assumed current model were adequate,

zₖ₊₁ = Mzₖ + Bd(zₖ)uₖ,   M = diag(exp(λⱼh)),
G(ℓh) ≈ Mℓ−1G(h),   ℓ ≥ 1.

After the first pulse hold, u is zero, so no choice of B can repair failure of the second line while M and the coordinates stay fixed. This test uses the measured first-hold G, not a fitted B. It therefore bypasses sparse coverage, regression conditioning and B parametrization as explanations for that failure.

A second test re-anchors the exponential after every sample in the buffer is post-pulse. The first such control times are 4.34 τ (long) and 0.06 τ (short). This is a diagnostic reset of the prediction, not a reset of the simulated trajectory and not an implemented controller.

3. What the pulses reveal

Late free trajectory: a strong separation of memory error from eigenfunction error

The principal example is free training record 1 at τ = 50.11, well into the eigenfunction’s valid late regime. The long-window fundamental reaches its largest response at 3.43 τ after the pulse, with a peak/first-hold ratio of 7,286.9. Its zero-input one-hold error is only about 0.003–0.004% over the later bands. Nevertheless, propagating the pulse’s first response fails at roughly 100% relative error. After 4.34 τ, the re-anchored complex exponential has 0.138% error over 4.34–8.67 τ and 0.203% over 8.67–14 τ.

Upper panels are log-y magnitudes, normalized by the first-hold response; the green prediction starts only when the window clears. Lower panels compare real parts for 4.32 τ after that point, so matching magnitudes alone cannot hide phase error. The short-window response peaks at 0.05 τ and is 11.7 times its first-hold response; its re-anchored prediction is useful but drifts more over longer horizons.

The echo responds immediately, and both latent coordinates begin responding immediately. Their response continues to change after the input is zero. This is the distinction between online availability and having a closed dynamical model for that online quantity.

Mild target preparation

Starting from one phase of the mild sine-driven target gives the same qualitative result. The long-window peak/first-hold ratio is 12,187; after the buffer clears, re-anchored errors are 0.21% and 0.41% in the two later bands. The prior sine is not continued: these are pulse-and-release tests from its history.

Nonlinear target preparation: clearing the window is not enough

The nonlinear example is different. Even the short-window zero-input continuation initially violates the fitted exponential strongly; the re-anchored pulse response remains poor. Thus memory is not the only failure: the late-trained, two-coordinate polynomial bank also lacks adequate validity over this strongly nonlinear region. Clearing the buffer is not a universal cure.

4. Refitting B from the pulses

For every nonempty subset of the three bank modes, all selected B rows were jointly refitted. The existing native affine-in-eigenfunctions/conjugates map, column scaling, SVD least squares and ridge 10⁻⁸ were reused:

Gⱼ(h) ≈ cⱼ₀ + Σℓ∈S [aⱼℓ Re(zℓ) + bⱼℓ Im(zℓ)],   j ∈ S.

There are 3, 5 or 7 real predictor columns for one, two or three complex modes. Each fit uses either free, mild, nonlinear, or pooled preparations. The dense campaign replaces mild/nonlinear sparse rows; its pooled fit combines both dense regimes plus the 12 free points without balancing the regimes. That weighting is important when interpreting its poor results.

The existing fitter accepts sampled transition residuals. To reuse it without altering its implementation, the experiment supplies a synthetic row with input 1 and next value Mz + G(h). Its residual is exactly G(h), up to a checked roundoff bound. This is a regression adapter, not a claim that a unit-amplitude pulse was measured. The fitted sampled coefficient is converted to the existing continuous map by dividing each row by (exp(λⱼh)−1)/λⱼ, exactly as before; this is the pre-existing frozen-map-in-a-hold approximation.

The two campaigns produced 98 distinct fit designs plus 14 deliberately repeated free-baseline fits (112 small map artifacts). Held-out records were never included in their own campaign’s training. The dense and sparse splits are different and explicitly recorded: sparse trains records 1,3,5; dense trains 1–6 and holds out 7–8. Do not interpret their validation scores as identical test sets.

5. Did pulse-fitted B improve actual control?

There were 40 closed-loop tests: 24 sparse-map and 16 dense-map trials; 37 completed and 3 stopped at native Riccati-solver failures. Control tests use ψ1 and ψ1+ψ2. All seven subsets were fitted and scored, but new three-mode control sweeps were not repeated: the prior experiment already showed the third mode’s poor free validity/driver behaviour, and the decisive structural test does not require it.

The controller is unchanged: normalized complex modes are split into real components, Q = I/m, R = ρ/0.01, a continuous CARE is solved at each 0.01 τ hold, u = −K(w−wtarget), and input is clipped at ±0.6. Feedback begins at 6 τ and runs to 60 τ. Both target and live coordinates use the same frozen causal transform. No target driver, state estimate, equations or future live echo enter feedback. The target driver is read only for post-run scoring. Target echo is used to define the reference and its RMS normalization, as before.

For a controlled comparison, ρ = 1 for short windows and 100 for long windows, matching the earlier displayed settings. These are not freshly optimized penalties. Results below use 30–60 τ, with final-10τ values in the appendix.

6. What is actually standing in the way?

  1. Forced histories leave the free-history relationship on which ψ was trained. For an unforced deterministic trajectory, a past window may be parameterized by its present physical state. Under forcing, the same current r,v can have different past echo/input histories. A pulse creates a mixed pre-/post-pulse window. A polynomial ψ fitted to free histories need not remain a Koopman eigenfunction on these histories.
  2. The current model assigns all unexplained change to the current input. In reality the projected pulse response continues evolving differently from Mz even after u = 0. Then a residual fit to continuously forced records can absorb correlations with previous inputs and drifting history into an “effective B.” That can be helpful for one periodic signal without being a true instantaneous causal input map.
  3. Pulse subtraction cleans up B estimation but does not add the missing dynamics. Fitting the true first-hold response may remove the accidental compensation that made a sine-trained effective map useful. That explains why a better causal calibration does not automatically improve closed-loop tracking.
  4. The nonlinear regime has an additional eigenfunction/domain problem. Early free errors, post-clean nonlinear errors and poor nonlinear B fits all persist. Two coordinates may also be insufficiently injective, but these tests do not separately quantify injectivity or prove its unique contribution. They establish failure of this compressed representation with its fitted generator, not impossibility of echo-only control.
  5. Pure error feedback is not exact reference tracking for a persistently forced target. If z equals ztarget exactly, the current law returns zero input. A target requiring a nonzero continuing sine cannot be exactly maintained at zero error by that law alone. This is a separate limitation from history memory; no feedforward or controller redesign was added here.

So your explanation was substantially right, with one important correction: we can see ξ and ψ online and can measure their immediate response. We are not blind for 8,644 samples. But the future response contains memory that is not represented by the assumed free generator plus instantaneous B. Continuous forcing does not make disentanglement mathematically impossible; the present compressed model and calibration objective do not account for it adequately.

The experiment cannot rule out a more appropriate set of echo-history observables, more latent coordinates, or a different dynamical realization. It also does not claim all sufficiently rich Koopman control frameworks fail. It shows that holding these ξ, ψ and M fixed and only refitting the present B is insufficient for a faithful pulse-response model, especially with the long window.

7. Recommended next steps, within your framework

  1. Keep this pulse test as a model-acceptance gate. Before control tuning, require useful unforced multi-step accuracy and a correct post-pulse response from the measured first-hold increment. Judge complex error and long-time behaviour, not just one-step residuals.
  2. Use the short window and ψ1/ψ2 in the mild regime as the current practical baseline. Keep the earlier sine-calibrated pair as the better demonstrated tracking controller. Label its B as an effective fitted map, not a validated causal state-Jacobian. Pulse maps are a diagnostic comparator, not a replacement that has earned deployment.
  3. Do not spend the next effort merely adding more sine waves or denser immediate B samples. The dense campaign has already removed sparse coverage as the sole explanation in the mild regime. Gain sweeps may improve tracking but cannot make G(ℓh) = Mℓ−1G(h) true when it is false.
  4. For stronger control, fix representation/closure first. Within the existing experimental policy, a next separately authorized test would repeat this same gate with four latent coordinates and eigenfunctions trained only on unforced echoes spanning the intended nonlinear domain. More coordinates may retain missing information, but they do not automatically make the currently assumed generator correct on mixed forced histories.
  5. If four coordinates still fail the pulse gate, explicitly decide whether memory belongs in the model. Possibilities include a state containing relevant history/input-history information or a pulse/delayed-response realization. These are future modelling choices, not changes made here. Waiting for a clean buffer and calling the resulting multi-window effect “instantaneous B” would mislabel the physics and would not fix the current controller.
  6. Only after the dynamical model passes, revisit persistent-reference tracking and laboratory robustness. Then consider the appropriate reference/feedforward treatment, measurement noise, finite actuator bandwidth and the intentionally omitted direct scattering. No claim of laboratory readiness follows from these noiseless numerical tests.
